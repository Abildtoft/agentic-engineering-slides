<!-- 14. section — The Compressing Middle -->

So the shift is real, and it is accelerating. That is the easy part, and it is
the part everyone argues about. The harder question is the one underneath it:
what exactly is changing about the work? Not the tools. The work. That is what
this next stretch is about, and I want to say up front that it is not only an
engineering question.

---

<!-- 15. default — The Middle of Software Work — ~3 clicks -->

Think of software work as having three parts. At one end, figuring out what to
build. At the other, the outcome it produces. And in between, a wide middle:
translating intent into a plan, implementing it, integrating it, coordinating
everyone involved.
[click] Karri Saarinen, who runs Linear, describes that middle as the place
that absorbed most of the time, attention and craft of software teams. For most
of us, the middle was the job.
[click] And it is where juniors learn. You grow up inside that middle. That is
where craft gets built, and I want to plant that thought now, because we will
come back to it.
[click] This is the part that is changing. Not disappearing, compressing. And
it is compressing on the product and design side too. The translation layer
between roles, the handoffs, the tickets that exist to carry intent from one
discipline to another. That was middle work as well. If the middle was 80% of
the effort, what happens when it drops to 20%?

---

<!-- 16. statement — The artifact was never the job. — ~2 clicks -->

The next sentence should sound slightly wrong for a second, after
everything I just said. The artifact was never the job.
[click] Duolingo's Chief Product Officer put it this way: a product manager
with a good idea can now get to a first draft faster than ever. They can visualise concepts,
test directions, move quickly. The distance between an idea and a thing you can
look at has collapsed.
[click] But the job was never the deck, or the prototype, or the code. The job
is deciding what should happen next. The artifact is just evidence that the
thinking happened. When one task was most of a job, automating the task can end
the job. If the job was the thinking, it just got faster.

<!-- 17. quote -->

Twenty-five years apart, two one-liners show how far the culture has moved.
Linus Torvalds, the creator of Linux, in 2000: talk is cheap, show me the code.
Half of us have that line somewhere in our hearts. I know I do.
[click] And then Kailash Nadh, the CTO of the Indian broker Zerodha, last
year: for the first time ever, good talk is exponentially more valuable than
good code. Talk here does not mean vague
ideation. It means a precise statement of the problem. That is now the scarce
thing, and it is scarce in every discipline, not just this one.

<!-- 18. quote — ~2 clicks -->

Jenny Wen leads design at Anthropic, the company building these agents, so she
is watching this from the inside. Here is the scene she describes. Engineers
spin up seven coding agents and ship a working version, before a designer has
finished exploring options.
[click] And her verdict: the classic discover, diverge, converge loop is
breaking down. That loop assumed implementation was the slow part, so you had
time to explore before anyone built anything. Take that assumption away and the
whole rhythm of collaboration changes.
[click] This is not anti-design. It is not engineering getting faster while
everyone else stands still. It is the operating model changing for every role
at once.

<!-- 19. default — One Surviving Skill Set — ~1 clicks -->

For a long time, product, design and engineering handed work to each other in
sequence. We always wanted it to be a Venn diagram. In practice it was a
waterfall. And the walls between the disciplines were built from one thing:
implementation was expensive, so you had to be careful about what reached it.
[click] Take that cost out, and the walls start to go with it. I watched it
happen in my own calendar the week I wrote this. I spent part of that week
programming product launch videos, while our media team was using an agent to
build a 3D web application from scratch. The engineer was doing media work,
the media team was doing engineering, and nobody had scheduled the swap. The
three disciplines do not collapse into one, but they overlap far more than
they used to.
[click] And what sits in the overlap is judgment. That is the thing that does
not compress. Some teams have a name for the person who lives there: the
product engineer, who frames the problem, ships the code and measures the
outcome. I do not think every company will adopt that title. I do think that
is the zone the market is rewarding.
[click] Three job titles. One job: turning ambiguity into clarity.

<!-- 20. default — The Bottleneck Cascade — ~3 clicks -->

There is an old pattern here, and it is worth seeing it in full.
[click] When weaving sped up, yarn became the constraint. When spinning caught
up, the constraint moved to raw cotton. And when the cotton gin flooded the
market with cotton, the constraint snapped back to weaving, and the power loom
answered it. It never ends. It circles.
[click] Every time a bottleneck is removed, the constraint moves upstream. Not
away. Upstream. The same thing is happening in radiology right now. Geoffrey
Hinton predicted AI would replace radiologists within five years. Instead,
salaries went up 44%, and AI mammography catches 29% more cancers. When the
cost collapsed, demand exploded, and the constraint moved to the humans
interpreting the results.
[click] So the middle is compressing, and the constraint is moving to clarity,
judgment and system design. Armin Ronacher has the operating principle: figure
out your bottleneck, automate it, then find the next bottleneck. That is the
new stack.
