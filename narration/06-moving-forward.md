<!-- 56. section — Moving Forward -->

We have covered the shift, the compressing middle, the new stack, and the debt
it can quietly build. One question is left, and it is the one the whole talk
has been circling: what actually survives?

<!-- 57. default — What Survives — ~3 clicks -->

Execution is increasingly abundant. People who combine strategy with
execution will remain rare. The same split plays out in engineering, product
and design.
[click] On the at-risk side: syntax and framework knowledge, routine
implementation, translation and handoff work, the speed of code production.
Not worthless. Less scarce. No longer the bottleneck.
[click] More valuable than ever: problem formulation and judgment. Taste, and
the courage to subtract. Systems thinking and end-to-end ownership. And
mentorship and knowledge transfer. None of this is new. It was always what the
best people did. Now it is the entire job. So if your value sits mostly on the
left, you have work to do. If you can own a problem end to end, the tools just
made you more powerful.
[click] This is not a tooling upgrade. It is a different operating model. Some
organisations are already here. Others are years behind. That is why the
industry feels so uneven right now.

---

<!-- 58. statement — Remember the cascade. The constraint moved upstream. — ~1 clicks -->

Remember the cascade from earlier? The constraint did not disappear. It moved
upstream, to getting expert knowledge out of heads and into systems, without
letting it disappear from the people accountable for it. The instruction
files, the skills, the docs: that is the knowledge-extraction mechanism. And it
is already reaching past engineers. The same primitives are being wrapped in
task-first interfaces so domain experts can operate them directly, while
engineers design the harness. Workflow design has
become cross-functional infrastructure.
[click] Satya Nadella has the ownership test: you should be able to swap the
model without losing the company-specific expertise. If swapping the model
wipes out your expertise, you did not own the learning system. The durable
asset is the institutional loop, not the frontier model.

---

<!-- 59. statement — In a period of compounding change, playing defense is its own risk. — ~2 clicks -->

Which makes posture the real decision. This is the divide from the opening: not
simply who has access, but who is learning. In a period of compounding change,
playing defense is its own risk.
[click] Protect what works. Wait for clarity. It feels safe. It isn't. The
real failure mode is not reckless shipping. It is strategic drift.
[click] Every new generation of agents makes today's optimal workflow
temporary, so do not overfit your organisation to the current model, the
current editor, the current orchestration pattern. Keep the harness strong,
the process light, and learn faster than the environment changes. The
emphasis is on learn, not on faster.

---

<!-- 60. default — Where to Start — ~3 clicks -->

Offense sounds abstract. On Monday morning it looks like this. You do not need
an AI strategy to begin. You need one finished pilot.
[click] Pick one workflow — that Monday-morning report from the long tail, or
whatever it is you assemble by hand every week. Recurring, annoying, low-risk.
Run it end to end,
pairing the person who knows the work with the person who can build. Domain
knowledge chooses the work, technical skill wires it up, and both review the
result. That pairing is the smallest complete learning loop.
[click] Keep a human in the loop while trust is being earned, and write down
what works. Prompts, playbooks, guardrails. Captured judgment is the asset
that compounds, and it is how one person's experiment becomes the
organisation's capability.
[click] Strategy is what you write down after the third pilot works. Not
before. Nobody wrote a winning internet strategy in 1997 either; the companies
that won just started building. A strategy written first is a document about
tools that will be obsolete before the ink dries.

<!-- 61. two-cols-header — The Next Few Years — ~2 clicks -->

And that posture lands differently depending on where you are in your career,
so let me split the audience for a moment.
[click] If you are earlier in your career: use AI to punch above your weight.
But understand and be able to explain every line, every prompt and every
decision you ship. That is a hard rule, not a nice-to-have. Seek out the
contexts where you build judgment, not just supervise output. And expand your
range. Simon Willison's advice is blunt: do not learn a new language, just
start writing code in it. He shipped three Go projects without studying Go.
The overhead that used to keep you in your lane is gone.
[click] If you are more senior: define value and multiply the team, not just
write code. If you only execute tickets, your leverage compresses. If you
define value and ship it, it expands. Lean on automation for the grunt work.
Create environments where juniors learn to reason, not just review AI output.
And invest in mentorship. All-senior teams do not scale tacit knowledge, and
the old apprenticeship model assumed a lot of routine middle work that is now
gone. This is the thread I planted at the start. It is the one I worry about
most.
